There is a very funny dispute on Wikipedia about the Coat of Arms of Canada.

Not funny because it is stupid, exactly. That would be too easy. It is funny because it is stupid in a way that has achieved institutional form.

Canada has a coat of arms. This is not, in ordinary life, a matter of live controversy. The Coat of Arms of Canada, also called the Arms of Canada or the Royal Arms of Canada, or if you wish to be formal the Arms of His Majesty The King in Right of Canada, was adopted by proclamation of King George V in 1921. It appears in official contexts. It is on government documents. It is on passports. It is one of those symbols most Canadians do not think about until they suddenly discover it has been quietly holding the constitutional order together from the cover of their travel document. Because I am annoying and this is relevant later:

> Tierced in fesse the first and second divisions containing the quarterly coat following, namely, 1st, gules three lions passant guardant in pale Or, 2nd, Or a lion rampant within a double tressure flory-counter-flory gules, 3rd, azure a harp Or stringed argent, 4th, azure, three fleurs-de-lis Or, and the third division argent three maple leaves conjoined on one stem proper. And upon a royal helmet mantled argent doubled gules the crest, that is to say, on a wreath of the colours argent and gules a lion passant guardant Or imperially crowned proper and holding in the dexter paw a maple leaf gules. And for supporters on the dexter a lion rampant Or holding a lance argent, point Or, flying therefrom to the dexter the Union Flag, and on the sinister, a unicorn argent armed crined and unguled Or, gorged with a coronet composed of crosses-patée and fleurs-de-lis a chain affixed thereto reflexed of the last, and holding a like lance flying therefrom to the sinister a banner azure charged with three fleurs-de-lis Or; the whole ensigned with the Imperial Crown proper and below the shield upon a wreath composed of roses, thistles, shamrocks and lillies a scroll azure inscribed with the motto A mari usque ad mare.

And yet, on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia%3AFiles_for_discussion/2025_March_2#File:Coat_of_arms_of_Canada.svg), the question of whether Canada’s coat of arms should appear in the infobox of the article about Canada has become one of those disputes that makes you realize civilization is both more fragile and more durable than you thought.

The dispute is not whether Canada has arms. [It does](https://www.gg.ca/en/heraldry/public-register/project/461). It is not even really about whether the coat of arms should be present in the infobox; country infoboxes commonly display coats of arms. The dispute is whether the Canada article should display them beside the flag, and if so, which version, under what copyright rationale, in what context, with what footnote, subject to which policy, after how many discussions, by whose consensus, and whether everyone involved has suffered enough.

This is where the absurdity begins. The official rendering of the arms is restricted. The Government of Canada says its official symbols, including design and colour variations, are protected against unauthorized use under the Trademarks Act, Copyright Act, and the Paris Convention. Wikipedia, meanwhile, has rules around non-free content. It is not supposed to use copyrighted images just because an image would be nice to have. That is generally good. An encyclopedia that treated “vibes” as a copyright exception would last approximately six minutes.

So the official rendering is allowed in the specific article about the Coat of Arms of Canada, where it is central to the subject. But using it more generally, such as in the Canada infobox, becomes harder to justify. A 2025 file discussion closed with the result: keep it in the Coat of Arms of Canada article, remove other instances.

Fine.

Except heraldry then enters the room, which is never a sign that a discussion is about to become simpler. A coat of arms is not quite a logo. In heraldry, the blazon is the formal written description of the arms. Different artists can draw different versions, called emblazonments, and still be heraldically correct if they follow the blazon. The Canadian Heraldic Authority’s register gives the blazon of the arms in words, which is exactly the sort of thing that makes heraldry people look at a copyright debate and begin vibrating at a frequency visible from space.

To a normal person, this sounds absurd. To a heraldry person, this is the entire point. To a Wikipedia editor, this is a policy problem with antlers.

One side says: use a free user-made rendition. It is not fake if it follows the blazon. Heraldry has worked like this for centuries. The official government image is only one artistic rendering of the arms.

The other side says: no, that risks misleading readers. Canada’s official symbol has an official protected form. A user-made version is not the same thing. If it looks too different, it is inaccurate. If it looks too similar, it may be infringing. Also the infobox is already bloated. Also please stop.

This is how you get a national-symbol argument conducted through phrases like "reasonable facsimile," "non-free content criteria," "infobox bloat," "official rendition," "free alternative," and "not the official court of arms," which is either a typo or a much funnier institution. The archive index is the real masterpiece. Someone has made an [index of the discussions about whether the coat of arms belongs in the Canada infobox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talk:Canada/Archive_index#Coat_of_arms_discussions). It begins in 2007 and continues through 2013, 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2022, 2025, and 2026. There are deletion discussions. There are related administrator discussions. There is a "derailed RfC, never resolved." There is a relisted file discussion that closed as no consensus. There are current talk-page sections, because of course there are.

This has somehow become a constitutional convention. The funniest line in the whole thing may be the exasperated realization that "long after we are all dead and buried, and our species has been replaced as the dominant one, there will still [be] a debate raging about wtf a 'Canada' is, or a 'coat of arms'." The cockroaches will inherit the Earth, open Wikipedia, and immediately propose a new RfC.

There is something deeply Canadian about all of this. Not because Canadians are uniquely polite or uniquely reasonable or any of the other comforting national lies we tell tourists and ourselves. It is Canadian because the whole thing is a small procedural crisis about the representation of sovereignty. A regular country might have a Wikipedia dispute about borders, capitals, wars, ethnic categories, presidents, disputed territories, or whether some deeply cursed historical figure should be called a liberator or a dictator.

Canada has an argument about whether its official arms may appear in a tiny box beside the flag, provided the image is either official but too copyrighted, unofficial but possibly correct, correct but potentially misleading, misleading but better than nothing, or nothing but arguably itself misleading.

That is Canada. A country that can turn sovereignty into a compliance question.

The arms themselves are already a strange object. They contain England, Scotland, Ireland, France, maple leaves, a royal crown, a lion, a unicorn, the motto “A Mari Usque Ad Mare,” and later the Order of Canada motto, because apparently the design was not already carrying enough institutional luggage. It is monarchy, federal identity, imperial inheritance, national symbolism, bilingual compromise, and passport aesthetics in one overloaded heraldic sandwich.

Then Wikipedia gets involved and asks: yes, but is this sandwich non-free content? The answer, apparently, is: please see previous discussions.

I don't even think one side is obviously deranged. That is the annoying part. The "show it" side has a point. It is weird for the main article about Canada not to show one of Canada's major official symbols. The coat of arms is not obscure in the way an old departmental badge is obscure. It appears in the visual language of the Canadian state. If readers see the flag but not the arms, some of them reasonably conclude Canada has no coat of arms, which is false.

The “don’t show it” side also has a point. Wikipedia’s non-free content rules exist for a reason. "It would be nice to have the picture" cannot become an all-purpose exception. The official image is restricted. User-made alternatives may raise questions of accuracy, labelling, and reader confusion. Even if I doubt Rideau Hall is going to bash Wikimedia's door down, I understand the caution. The law is not vibes. Copyright is not vibes. Heraldry, unfortunately, is also not vibes.

That is why the dispute is perfect. Nobody is merely wrong enough for the argument to die. Instead, it lives forever in the place all immortal procedural arguments live: the `talk` page.

The Canada article is already one of those pages that has to summarize a country, a state, a constitutional monarchy, a federation, a geography, a history, a culture, an economy, a demographic reality, a legal order, and several unresolved national psychodramas without becoming a 400,000-word civic landfill. Somewhere inside that constraint, a 200-pixel heraldic image has become a proxy war over law, symbolism, accuracy, copyright, and whether infoboxes are useful or a disease.

This is what I love about Wikipedia. It is an encyclopedia, yes. But it is also a museum of arguments. Not just what we know, but how we fight about what it means to say we know it. Canada has a coat of arms. Wikipedia has a process. Between them is a `talk` page, a file discussion, a copyright policy, a free rendering, a government restriction, an exhausted editor, a heraldry enthusiast, and a unicorn chained to a procedural bomb.

*A Mari Usque Ad Mare* means "from sea to sea."

*A Mari Usque ad Infobox* means "from sea to infobox."

Which, in fairness, is exactly the disputed route.
