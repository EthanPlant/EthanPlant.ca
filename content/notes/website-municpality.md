My website has appears to have developed what I can only describe as a government.

This was not the plan. The plan, to the extent one existed, was fairly normal. I wanted a website. A place to put my writing. A small, plain, reasonably durable bit of the web I owned. Static files. An [RSS feed](/rss.xml). [A colophon](/colophon/). A few [project pages](/projects). Maybe a note every so often when a federal bill, software platform, or dropdown menu became too annoying to leave alone. That was already dangerous.

Then I started seeing the website as a home. Not in the inspirational software sense, where a product manager says "home" and means a dashboard with a retention strategy. I mean home in the older and more literal web sense. A place with an address. A front door. Rooms. Shelves. A basement full of cables and regrets. A little sign out front saying, in effect, this is where I live on the internet.

Then a few weeks ago I added TownSquare, and jokingly referred to it as a [porch](/notes/townsquare/). This is where the absurdity began.

[TownSquare](https://townsquare.cauenapier.com/) is a little widget that puts a tiny public square on your website. Visitors can appear as small wandering figures. They can say hello. There are benches. There is a lamp post. There is a question mark button, because even the miniature civic realm apparently needs a help desk. It's extremely charming, it is also the kind of thing that immediately turns a personal website from "an archive of writing" into "a place where someone might loiter."

This is not a complaint. I love it. I added it on purpose. I looked at a quiet little corner of the web and thought: yes, what this needs is a public square administered by one guy in Vancouver with strong opinions about RSS.

That would have been enough for me to be happy. Then TownSquare recently added a message board feature, a little box that lets me put a note for visitors that is rendered in the widget as a cute little sign. Naturally, I used it responsibly:

> This website now has a message board because apparently a personal website was not already enough of a public liability.

This is stupid. It is also the exact right amount of stupid. The board now appears at the bottom of pages about Canadian policy, privacy machinery, and whatever civic infrastructure brainworm has crawled into my head this week. Below the essays, above the footer, there is a tiny municipal bulletin telling people to behave as though they are standing in a town square maintained by a Canadian infrastructure engineer with no municipal budget.

Which is, unfortunately, increasingly accurate. 

A personal website has a records office. That is the archive.

It has a public works department. That is the build system, CSS, DNS, feeds, redirects, and all the boring little bits that keep the place from falling into the ocean.

It has a planning department. That is the roadmap of projects I keep pretending are separate from each other.

It has a transportation department. That is [Elsewhere](/projects/elsewhere/), taking writing from the home address and moving it outward to the platform suburbs without pretending the suburbs are the city centre.

It has a public notice board now. Please stop me before I start adding bylaws.

The joke is that this is ridiculous. It is completely absurd to think of a website through a metaphor of civic government. The serious part is that it reveals something I think the platform web has spent twenty years trying to make us forget: a place needs governance. Not "governance" in the slide-deck sense. Not stakeholder alignment. Not community guidelines written by a trust-and-safety department that reports to growth. I mean the plain relationship between authority and responsibility.

Who can change the thing? Who has to answer when it breaks? Who decides what belongs there? Who keeps the records? Who can remove someone? Who is accountable for the space existing at all?

On a platform, those questions are deliberately smeared across a stack of policy documents, moderation queues, ranking systems, shareholder incentives, and support forms that go nowhere. The user is told they have a profile, a page, a creator presence, a community, a brand, a following. They are very rarely told the simple thing: [you are renting behaviour from someone else’s machine](/writing/access-is-not-ownership/).

On a personal website, the answer is much stupider and much clearer. Me. I can change it. 

I can break it. I can fix it. I can publish the note. I can delete the note. I can add the tiny public square. I can remove the tiny public square. I can decide the footer should say "No cookies. No trackers. Just writing." I can decide the site is written in [Canadian English because it is my site and Canada is a real place, despite the internet’s ongoing difficulty with this concept](/notes/en-ca/).

This is not a perfect model. Personal rule is not democracy. A website is not a province. The Governor General isn't going to ask whether my sidebar commands the confidence of the homepage, although at this point I refuse to rule out a constitutional crisis involving the RSS feed.

But the relationship between power and responsibility is legible. That's the part the platform web keeps dissolving.

Increasingly I’ve come to believe that a personal website is a political act. Not in the partisan sense. Not in the yard-sign, donation-email, everyone-is-yelling sense. Political in the older sense: concerned with how people live together, where authority sits, who owns the means of publication, what kind of public life is possible, and whether a person is allowed to have a home address on the internet that is not mediated by a company pretending its landlord powers are community features.

People are going to disagree with this. The tech industry gets twitchy around the word "political" because it prefers to describe power in friendlier terms, like platform policy, user experience, trust and safety, monetization, or "the ecosystem". Fine. The argument still stands.

A website as a home is already a claim about ownership. It says the centre of gravity is here. The platforms are edges. Mastodon, Bluesky, Hacker News, email, feeds, search engines, group chats, screenshots sent to friends with “please stop” attached, all of these can be distribution networks, but none of them are the house. The house is the place with the address. A website as a municipality is the next, worse thought. It says the home has acquired public functions. People can arrive. People can read notices. People can leave messages. People can move through the little square and interact with a space that is not quite private and not quite public in the old way.

TownSquare's creator appears to also understand the joke. The project's blog is called *The Town Square Gazette*, which would already be bad enough if it were merely a cute name for a product blog. But the Gazette notices themselves are written entirely in the bit. [One notice](https://townsquare.cauenapier.com/blog/posts/map-roads-gazette/) describes map routing improvements as though the town cartographers had been retrained after previously drawing straight-line "accusations" across forests, lakes, mountains, and other people’s vegetable patches. It refers readers to "evidence submitted to the public," discusses roads learning to go around things, and closes with matters still under review. 

The Gazette is also doing town highlights, because apparently the local paper has discovered human-interest journalism. [One entry](https://townsquare.cauenapier.com/blog/posts/town-highlight_1/) opens with a reporter visiting "a quiet town square somewhere on the web," then proceeds to describe benches, trees, birds, and the proprietor of the site as though this is a normal beat assignment. It interviews [Omar](https://lowsound.dev/), a Toronto web developer who works for the municipal government building tools for Transportation engineers and urban planners, which is so perfectly on the nose that it feels legally unsafe to invent. The fake civic newspaper for the tiny website-square widget interviewed an actual municipal-adjacent small-web person about his personal site.

Then, because the bit has discipline, it ends with a disclaimer that opinions expressed in interviews belong to their respective authors and do not necessarily reflect the views of the TownSquare team. This is not merely a cute blog post. This is a local paper with an editorial policy. The Gazette has reporters, residents, town highlights, infrastructure notices, and institutional distance from its interview subjects. This is how municipalities happen. First there is a square. Then there is a notice board. Then there is a Gazette. Then the Gazette starts profiling residents and disclaiming editorial responsibility. At that point you are not running a widget anymore, you are maintaining civic media. A person can only be expected to endure so much constitutional symbolism from JavaScript.

This is where responsible government sneaks in through the side door wearing a fake moustache. Responsible government is, stripped down, the idea that executive power has to remain answerable to the representative body it depends on. Authority cannot just float above the system, issuing commands from a decorative balcony. It has to be tied to confidence, procedure, and consequence.

A personal website doesn't have a Parliament obviously, which is probably for the best because I do not want to discover what Question Period looks like in my comments. But it can have a tiny version of the same principle: the people who operate the system should be the people accountable for what the system does.

If I put a public square on my website, I don't get to pretend the square is someone else’s problem. If I add a message board, I don't get to act surprised when the website starts to feel like it has notices, norms, and a vaguely cursed civic identity. If I invite people into a space, even a small and silly one, I have responsibilities toward that space.

Not grand ones. Not state power. Not a charter of rights for the bench widget. Basic ones. Don't trick people. Don't spy on people. Don't make the interface say one thing while the architecture says another. Don't pretend an owned home and a rented platform are the same because both have a textbox. Don't build a public square and then act like nobody is responsible for sweeping it.

This is the part where the joke starts sounding less like a joke. The small web is often described in aesthetic terms. Cozy. Handmade. Weird. Personal. Slow. These are fine words, although "cozy" starts to sound threatening after enough people use it to describe beige software with rounded corners.

The better argument for the small web though is not that it is cute, even if it often is. The better argument is that it makes authority visible. A static website is boring in a useful way. The files are there. The links are there. The feed is there. The source can be understood. The owner can be found. The failure modes are often crude, but at least they are honest. A broken link is annoying; a platform silently deciding your work is no longer eligible for distribution because a machine disliked the shape of it is governance wearing a hoodie and pretending to be weather.

The web should have more places where the rules are simple enough to inspect, the owner is close enough to answer, and the machinery is boring enough to trust. This doesn't mean every website needs a town square. Most websites absolutely do not need a town square. Many websites barely deserve a contact form. The internet has already produced enough comment sections to prove that "letting people type things" is not, by itself, a theory of civic life. 

But a personal website should be allowed to become more than a content bucket or boring portfolio. It should be allowed to have a porch, or a notice board. It should be allowed to have a messy archive, a links page, a feed, a style, a local vocabulary, and a few procedural oddities that only make sense because a real person lives there.

It should be allowed to sound like it came from somewhere.

Mine, apparently, came from Vancouver, passed through Ottawa, picked up a working knowledge of public institutions, developed a fixation on static HTML, and has now begun forming municipal organs. This is a concerning development, but still, I trust it more than the alternative.

The alternative is a web where every home has been converted into tenant space inside someone else’s commercial district. You may decorate the unit. You may invite guests. You may post notices, subject to policy. You may build an audience, subject to ranking. You may preserve your work, subject to export tools. You may speak, subject to the landlord’s evolving theory of acceptable speech, acceptable links, acceptable identity, acceptable automation, acceptable politics, acceptable everything. Everyone calls this convenience because "managed dependency on opaque private infrastructure" doesn't test well in a focus group..

Personally though, I want the address. I want the files. I want the feed. I want the public square to be a weird little widget I chose, not a growth surface I was assigned. I want the message board to be funny because I wrote the notice, not because a platform’s engagement team discovered "community" in a quarterly planning cycle and immediately made it worse.

This website started as a place to put writing. Then it became a home. Now, somehow, it has a functioning theory of responsible government: authority should be visible, responsibility should be local, and the person running the system should not get to hide behind the system when the system acts.

So yes. My website is slowly becoming a municipality. This is ridiculous. It's also real.
