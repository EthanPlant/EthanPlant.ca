One of the stranger pleasures of having a personal website is seeing how it moves around the web without you.

I was looking at this site's analytics and noticed I had a surprising amount of traffic coming from [Kagi](https://kagi.com). Nothing massive, but definitely persistent. At first I assumed it was just search traffic.

![Screenshot of analytics showing hits from Kagi](/images/kagi.png)

Then I discovered the reason. This site had been added to [Kagi's Small Web](https://kagi.com/smallweb/) feed. And that feels... oddly perfect.

I built this site to be my own small piece on the web. Static pages. RSS. Writing. Notes. Projects. A colophon. A few unfinished rooms. No popups. No trackers. No attempt to turn every visitor into a conversion event. And somehow it was found by a system explicitly interested in small, personal, human-scale websites.

Something about that is quietly encouraging.

A lot of the modern web teaches you to think discovery must come through platforms. Post to the feed. Optimize the title. Build the audience. Capture the email. Improve the funnel. Measure the retention. But this was something else.

This was the web doing one of the things it's still very good at when you let it: finding a page, following a feed, adding a link, and quietly making one small place visible from another. That feels much closer to the web I actually like. Not viral. Not optimized. Not algorithmically forced into someone’s attention.

Just... discovered.

A personal site can honestly feel lonely at first. You make the pages, fix the CSS, get RSS working, watch bots ask if you are WordPress, and wonder whether any of it exists outside your own little maintenance loop.

Then one day you find your site in someone else’s small-web list. A tiny, yet real proof of life. 

The web noticed.
