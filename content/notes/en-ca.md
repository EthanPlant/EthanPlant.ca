Terence Eden recently wrote a lovely little post called "[This blog is written in en-GB](https://shkspr.mobi/blog/2026/07/this-blog-is-written-in-en-gb/)".

The setup is simple. A reader asked if he would mind making his language more inclusive because they didn't understand some of the British cultural references. Perhaps, the reader suggested, he could use tropes that were more globally familiar.

His answer, correctly, was no.

Not "no" in the sense of being needlessly hostile to readers. Not "no" in the sense that writers should deliberately obscure things for fun. Just no in the ordinary, healthy, necessary sense that a personal website is allowed to come from somewhere.

His blog declares itself as `en-GB` because that isn't just the spelling system he happens to use. It's the cultural context he writes from. The accent, the references, the assumptions, the jokes, the shape of the sentences, and the particular collection of small familiar things that make his writing his.

Which, naturally, made me think about this website.

This website is written in `en-CA`, check the HTML if you wish. Not American English with snow tires. Not British English with worse weather and better constitutional ambiguity. Not "neutral global English" with the occasional "u" left in "colour" as a treat. Canadian English.

This matters to me for the same reason I deliberately chose a `.ca` domain name. The domain isn't just a routing decision. It's a statement of place. It says this website is Canadian before you read a word. It says the home of this thing is not a platform, not a feed, not a corporate profile, and not some frictionless globally optimized content object. It is a website, written by a Canadian, from Canada, under a Canadian domain.

That does not mean every post is about Canada. It does not mean every reader needs to arrive with a working knowledge of Responsible Government, TransLink, Heritage Minutes, the Canada Line, federalism, the Charter, CBC panel shows, or the emotional significance of hearing a city lose its mind because the national team scored at home.

But it does mean I am not going to sand all of that off so the internet can pretend every sentence was written from nowhere.

There is a small irony here. A lot of requests to make writing "more inclusive" are, in practice, requests for the writer to become more familiar to the requester. Not always. Sometimes inclusion means noticing an avoidable barrier and removing it. That is good. That is part of being a considerate person. If a sentence is needlessly confusing, make it clearer. If a reader needs context, provide enough context to help them follow. If a word is doing harm rather than work, choose another word.

But not every unfamiliar thing is a barrier. Sometimes it's just unfamiliar, and that's okay. A British reference in a British blog isn't an exclusionary act. A Canadian reference on a Canadian website isn't a failure of accessibility. A local idiom isn't an obstacle simply because someone elsewhere has to infer its meaning from context. The reader encountering something outside their own cultural defaults is not being harmed. They are reading.

There is a funny way in which "please make this more inclusive" can become "please modify your behaviour to match my preference for what inclusive language should look like." At that point, inclusion has become a demand for conformity with better branding.

This is what Terence's post catches so well. The blog is not refusing readers, it's just refusing to pretend it was written from nowhere. There is a difference between clarity and cultural laundering.

This website will contain Canadian references because this website is Canadian. Sometimes it will contain phrases like "the Crown," "the Governor General," "confidence," "on division," "the House," "the Senate," "Rideau Hall," "the notwithstanding clause," "the Canada Gazette," "Hydro," "Terry Fox," "the King in Right of Canada," and "God save our King and Heaven bless."

[Sometimes it will contain sentences that sound perfectly normal to me and deranged to everyone else](/notes/canada-day-is-coming/), such as:
> Canada Day is coming, the World Cup is here, and Vancouver may be one draw away from becoming the loudest civic logistics problem in Canadian history.

Sometimes it will assume you understand that Waterfront Station is less of a station and more of a psychological experiment.

Sometimes it will describe Vancouver as a city of fireworks, concerts, cruise ships, Stanley Cup trauma, beach weather, Canucks hope, Olympic legacy, and the annual civic ritual of pretending the Canada Line was built for the number of people who actually use it.

Sometimes it will say that a 6–0 win over Qatar in Vancouver made the city sound like it had briefly remembered it was allowed to feel things.

Sometimes it will observe that Canada Day can feel awkward, staged, laminated, lovely, chaotic, sincere, and deeply funny all at once.

Sometimes it will say "this is how you accidentally create a Heritage Moment" and expect you to either know what that means or enjoy figuring it out from context.

Sometimes it will say:

> May God help the poor person who thinks they can casually transfer at Waterfront.

> May God especially help the Canada Line.

> She was not built for this.

> But she will be asked to serve.

That is `en-CA`.

Not merely the spelling. Not merely "centre", "colour" and "labour". Though yes, those too. It's the references, the institutions, the jokes, the implied civic trauma. The soft national embarrassment. The way public transit becomes a character. The way constitutional monarchy can be both very funny and not at all imaginary. The way Canada Day can be sincere and weird and laminated and moving and faintly uncomfortable at the same time.

It is the particular Canadian ability to insist one is not nationalistic and then become emotionally compromised by a hockey game, a World Cup goal, a Gord Downie lyric, a Terry Fox statue, a Heritage Minute, or the sight of a maple leaf being used tastefully for once.

This is not a defect in the writing or me deliberately trying to be obtuse. It's just the frame of reference I write from, the soil it grows out of if we're feeling poetic.

The internet has a habit of treating American cultural context as neutral and everyone else's cultural context as flavour. American spellings are default. American politics are default. American assumptions about courts, presidents, speech, rights, flags, police, religion, taxes, guns, elections, and geography are treated as the common operating system of reality. Everyone else is expected to provide localization files.

No. This website is not localized American content with extra "u"s in words. This website is written in `en-CA`. That means "Hydro" may mean electricity. "College" and "university" are not interchangeable. "The Crown" is not a metaphor. "The government" may mean the ministry, the state, the public service, the governing party, or whichever poor communications team is about to have a bad afternoon.

It means a Prime Minister can lose office without a general election.

It means a Prime Minister can become Prime Minister before winning a seat in the House.

It means the government can survive because enough opposition MPs abstain.

It means a bill can pass "on division," which means the House agrees without a recorded vote while people are still mad about it.

It means the Governor General can ask whether the Prime Minister actually commands confidence.

It means the King technically has enormous reserve powers that he must almost certainly never use because everyone would get very upset.

It means the Senate can amend legislation but is unelected and therefore must be careful about pretending it is not unelected.

It means the House of Commons ceremonially slams the door in the face of the Crown's messenger to demonstrate its independence, which sounds like something made up by a theatre kid with a political science minor, but is in fact a normal part of opening Parliament.

Context will usually make it clear. If it does not, welcome. That is how reading works.

This is also why the `.ca` matters. A domain is not merely branding. It is jurisdiction, identity, and geography compressed into a suffix. It is a tiny flag planted in DNS. I do not mean that in a chest-thumping way. I mean it in the ordinary civic way. This is where I am. This is the public context I live in. This is the political vocabulary I inherited. This is the set of institutions that shaped my understanding of how power works. This is the country whose arguments I understand well enough to be annoyed by them properly.

The small web is better when it sounds like people. Not content verticals. Not globally optimized engagement units. Not pasteurized thought product. Not copy in a hero section that's been smeared of all substance by a marketing team. People.

People from Britain writing in `en-GB`. People from Ireland writing in `en-IE`. People from India, Ghana, Singapore, Australia, New Zealand, Nigeria, South Africa, Jamaica, Malaysia, the Philippines, and everywhere else English has been adopted, bent, inherited, imposed, remixed, localized, and made strange in useful ways.

And yes, people from Canada writing in `en-CA`.

Some of my references will be Canadian. Some will be Ontarian. Some will be British Columbian. Some will be Vancouver-specific enough to cause psychic damage to anyone who has ever tried to transfer at Waterfront during a major event. Some will make perfect sense to three people, one of whom works at TransLink. Some will involve the Crown in ways that make Americans ask whether I am doing a bit.

I am usually doing a bit. If you want serious commentary on the Canadian constitutional order, this is probably not the footnote you should cite.

The Crown is also real. That tension is, unfortunately, part of the dialect.

And because this is the small web, there is also the funniest possible escalation available: I can write this note, link to Terence’s post, publish it on my `.ca` domain, and quietly send a Webmention. Not a quote-tweet. Not a dunk. Not an algorithmic reply. Not a post fired into the social-media machine in the hope that a platform briefly decides we are allowed to perceive each other.

A Webmention. A tiny little "hello, I wrote something in response to this" sent from one website to another website, like a diplomatic note delivered by a very polite protocol goblin.

This is, admittedly, an absurd amount of infrastructure for saying "yes, exactly, and also Canada." But that is part of the charm. A personal website should be allowed to behave like a person. It should be allowed to answer another website from its own front porch. It should be allowed to have an accent, a country, a transit system, a constitutional vocabulary, and a set of references that are not immediately legible to everyone on Earth.

So yes.

This website is written in `en-CA`.

Reader discretion is advised. You may encounter Westminster procedure, federal-provincial resentment, public broadcasting, bilingual label energy, transit fatalism, constitutional monarchy, hockey trauma, regional grievance, maple symbolism, and the creeping realization that Canada is not merely America with healthcare.

You will be fine. Context clues are available.
