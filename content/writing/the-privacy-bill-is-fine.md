It would be convenient if [Bill C-36](https://www.parl.ca/DocumentViewer/en/45-1/bill/C-36/first-reading) were obviously bad.

It would be much easier to write about it if it were a stupid bill. It would be much easier if it were the sort of thing you could point at, sigh, and say, "well, there it is, the government has once again decided the internet is a machine for doing crimes and the solution is a registry, a regulator, and a prayer."

But that is not really what Bill C-36 is.

Bill C-36 is, at least on its face, a privacy bill. More specifically, it is the federal government's latest attempt to modernize Canada's private-sector privacy law. It would enact the *Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act*, repeal Part 1 of PIPEDA, and leave the old electronic-documents machinery behind as the renamed *Electronic Documents Act*. It was introduced at first reading on June 15, 2026, by the Minister of Artificial Intelligence and Digital Innovation.

That is not an inherently absurd thing to do. In fact, in broad strokes, Canada should probably have done it years ago. PIPEDA is old enough that calling it a privacy law for the modern internet feels increasingly like calling Confederation a contemporary housing strategy. It belongs to a world where "electronic documents" sounded like the future. That doesn't mean it was useless. It does mean it was built before the modern platform economy, before smartphones became prosthetic organs, before data brokers became ambient infrastructure, before machine-learning systems became a normal part of consumer decision-making, and before every second company decided that "personalization" was a polite word for "[we built a weird little dossier about you.](/writing/bridger/)"

On that front, C-36 is not nothing. The bill recognizes privacy as a fundamental right with respect to personal information, while also acknowledging that organizations need to collect, use, and disclose personal information for reasonable purposes. It also makes clear that de-identified personal information does not stop being personal information merely because it has been de-identified.

Good.

That is exactly the kind of sentence modern privacy law needs. The entire digital economy is full of companies trying to convince themselves that once they have transformed data enough, abstracted it enough, bucketed it enough, hashed it enough, or fed it through enough "insights" machinery, the privacy problem has somehow evaporated. It should go without saying that it has not.

C-36 also defines sensitive information in a way that includes children's personal information, racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic information, health information, biometric information capable of uniquely identifying an individual, and information concerning sexual orientation.

Again: good.

If an app asks users for religion, sexuality, ethnicity, health-adjacent information, family information, and children’s information, the law should not treat that data as if it were morally equivalent to a dark-mode preference. Some data has a larger blast radius. Some data can become dangerous in the wrong context. Some data can be used for discrimination, coercion, harassment, phishing, impersonation, stalking, or social engineering. A privacy law that cannot see that difference is not serious.

C-36 also contains practical obligations that are, frankly, boring in the way good laws are often boring. Organizations have to be accountable for personal information under their control. They have to designate someone responsible for privacy obligations. They have to maintain a privacy management program. They have to use safeguards proportionate to the sensitivity of the information. They have to avoid retaining personal information longer than necessary. They have to dispose of personal information in certain circumstances.

Again, begrudgingly: good.

A privacy law should contain many boring sentences that make compliance departments nod grimly and open spreadsheets. That is not a criticism. That is civilization.

The bill also gives individuals access rights. If an organization has personal information about someone, the person can ask whether the organization has it, how it uses it, whether it has disclosed it, and to whom or to what types of third parties. If an automated decision system has made a prediction, recommendation, or decision about the individual with legal or similarly significant effect, the organization must provide an explanation on request, including the type of personal information used, its source, and the principal factors behind the decision.

Once again: good.

People should be able to know when consequential decisions about them are being made by automated systems. They should be able to ask what information went into those systems. They should be able to challenge inaccurate information. They should be able to withdraw consent where meaningful. They should not have to beg a chatbot, escalate through three support queues, threaten to email the regulator, and then receive a PDF written by a legal department that has clearly never met a human being.

Even the enforcement structure is not obviously absurd. C-36 sets the maximum administrative monetary penalty for contraventions found after one investigation at the greater of $10 million or 3% of the organization’s gross global revenue in the previous financial year.

Entirely reasonable. A privacy law without meaningful enforcement is not a law. It is a strongly worded suggestion with a filing cabinet attached. If the cost of violating privacy law is a rounding error, the law is not regulating the company. The company is pricing the law.

So no, the problem with C-36 is not that privacy modernization is bad. I can happily concede the government has done its homework here.

The problem is the machine being assembled around it. 

And here is where the argument becomes much less abstract: Canada did not need to invent a privacy regulator. It already has one. The Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada exists. It is an Agent of Parliament whose mission is to protect and promote privacy rights. The Privacy Commissioner has institutional memory. It has privacy-specific expertise. It has credibility. It already understands the private-sector privacy file because that file has been part of its job under PIPEDA for decades. If the problem was that Canadian privacy law lacked teeth, the obvious answer was to give the Privacy Commissioner sharper teeth.

Give the office order-making power. Give it meaningful penalties. Give it modern statutory tools. Give it the budget, staff, and authority required to enforce a serious privacy law in a modern digital economy. That would have been a straightforward institutional story: Canada’s privacy law is outdated, so Canada is modernizing the law and strengthening the existing privacy watchdog. That'd be the kind of boring Canadian story that five people care about and I'm unfortunately one of them.

C-36, unfortunately, does something stranger. C-36 does not simply create a modernized private-sector privacy law and hand it to the existing privacy regulator. It takes the Digital Safety Commission of Canada created by [Bill C-34](https://www.parl.ca/DocumentViewer/en/45-1/bill/C-34/first-reading) and turns it into the Digital Safety and Data Protection Commission of Canada. Clause 8 of C-36 literally replaces the long title of the *Digital Safety Commission of Canada Act* so that it becomes “An Act to establish the Digital Safety and Data Protection Commission of Canada.” Later clauses rename the Commission itself and give it powers, duties, and functions under the *Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act*.

Private-sector privacy is not merely modernized. It is moved into the same new institutional architecture being built for online safety, platform duties, age assurance, synthetic content, AI chatbots, and harmful content.

But the procedural absurdity goes one layer deeper.

Because, my dear reader, The *Digital Safety Commission of Canada Act* does not exist yet. It is still a bill in front of Parliament. 

C-34, introduced on June 10, 2026, is the bill that would enact the *Digital Safety Act* and the *Digital Safety Commission of Canada Act*. It is currently at second reading in the House of Commons, with no activity listed at second reading as of the parliamentary page I checked. Then, five days after C-34 was introduced, C-36 arrived and said: once that bill creates the thing, this bill will rename the thing, expand the thing, and use the thing as the foundation for Canada’s private-sector privacy regime.

Legally, I understand what is happening. These are conditional and coordinating amendments. C-36 even says the quiet part in procedural language: if Bill C-34 receives royal assent, Part 3 of C-36 comes into force by order in council, but not before section 4 of the *Digital Safety Commission of Canada Act*, as enacted by C-34, comes into force. In normal terms, "Once that bill becomes law, this part of this bill also becomes law."

So no, strictly speaking, Parliament is not amending an Act that does not exist. It is drafting a bill that conditionally amends an Act that would exist if another bill passes first. Fine. That is procedurally explainable. It is also exactly the problem.

C-34 is not an old statute that needs downstream cleanup. It is not some settled piece of legislative furniture. It is a separate, controversial bill currently before Parliament. It would create a new online harms and digital safety regulator. Whether that regulator should exist, what powers it should have, how it should handle age assurance, how it should interact with platforms, and how it should be constrained are all live democratic questions. Yet C-36 is already treating C-34’s regulator as reusable machinery.

That is the part that I have to draw the line at. Parliament is being asked to debate C-34 as one bill and C-36 as another bill, while the government’s real architecture only becomes visible when the bills are stacked together. C-34 builds the chassis. C-36 bolts privacy onto it.

In Ottawa, many bad ideas begin life as something that sounds efficient. The charitable explanation is that C-36 is a separate privacy bill, and adding the entire private-sector privacy regime to C-34 would have made C-34 even more sprawling than it already is. Fine. Privacy modernization deserves its own bill. Nobody serious wants the *Safe Social Media Act* to become the *Safe Social Media and Also All of Private-Sector Privacy Act*. 

But, if privacy deserves its own bill, privacy also deserves its own institutional logic. Instead, C-36 treats the C-34 commission as if it were already part of the constitutional furniture. It takes a proposed online harms regulator, before Parliament has approved it, and repurposes it as the home for private-sector privacy enforcement. That is a major rights regime being designed as a dependency of another still-contested regime.

Michael Geist has been making this point sharply. [His critique](https://www.michaelgeist.ca/2026/06/canadas-digital-super-regulator-bill-c-36-pushes-out-the-privacy-commissioner-and-hands-private-sector-privacy-to-an-overloaded-commission/) is that C-36 pushes private-sector privacy enforcement away from the Privacy Commissioner and hands it to a new digital super-regulator: the same commission the government created days earlier to police online harms. He also notes the obvious but important point that private-sector privacy law is not just a digital-platform file. It governs how banks, airlines, insurers, retailers, and many other organizations handle personal information in contexts that have nothing to do with social media or chatbots.

This is obviously true. My pharmacy mishandling health-adjacent personal information is not spiritually the same problem as TikTok moderation. My bank's privacy obligations are not the same kind of issue as a chatbot's crisis-response duties. A retailer's loyalty program, an airline's passenger records, an insurer's risk models, an employer's applicant data, and a close-friends app's weird little identity dossier are all privacy issues. Some are digital. Some are not. All of them belong to an economy-wide rights and accountability regime.

Why should that whole file be folded into the online harms regulator?

The answer, presumably, is coherence. Digital safety and data protection overlap. Age verification creates privacy risks. Harmful-content systems process personal information. Automated moderation, synthetic-content labelling, recommender systems, chatbot safeguards, platform transparency, and child-safety duties all involve data. A regulator that understands technical systems could, in theory, handle these overlaps better than a fragmented institutional soup where every agency owns one square inch of the elephant.

That is the generous read. The less generous version is that Canada is building a general-purpose digital governance machine and feeding it every problem that has the word "online" somewhere in the briefing note. Privacy. Online safety. Age assurance. AI chatbots. Synthetic content. Harmful content. Children's safety. Platform transparency. Cross-border data transfers. Automated decision-making. Data mobility. Lawful access in the background. CRTC coordination. Competition coordination. Provincial coordination. Foreign regulator coordination.

Individually, many of these problems are real. Together, they look less like careful lawmaking and more like the federal government slowly discovering that the internet exists and deciding the answer is one giant dashboard.

This is the part that makes me tired. Not because the state should do nothing. Not because platforms are good. Not because privacy law should remain frozen in 2000. Not because children are imaginary. Not because AI chatbot services should be allowed to operate as unaccountable emotional slot machines pointed at teenagers. The problem is that Canadian digital policy keeps collapsing distinct questions into the same institutional reflex.

Something bad happens online. Parliament identifies a real harm. The government introduces a bill. The bill creates a regulator, assigns broad duties, leaves large chunks to regulation, relies on assurances about proportionality and safeguards, and then insists that everyone worried about the machinery must not care about the harm.

That is a miserable way to make law. It's also not even clear that the machinery gets us privacy rights faster.

[Geist's other major criticism is the delay.](https://www.michaelgeist.ca/2026/06/new-rights-new-powers-long-delays-bill-c-36s-seven-step-process-for-privacy-reform-to-take-effect/) His view is that C-36 places its new rights and powers behind a Commission that does not exist yet, a rulebook that has yet to be written, a privacy office that hasn't been filled, and regulations that haven't been drafted. He also notes that some rights and powers depend on further regulations even after the law is in force. [His estimate is that the new privacy legislation won't come into effect until the 2030s](https://www.michaelgeist.ca/2026/06/billc36/):

> Rather than getting a new privacy law in effect in 2027, a more likely scenario means waiting until 2030 or 2031 for an untested, inexperienced new regulator.

This is remarkable. The government says privacy is urgent, then designs a process where privacy reform depends on an unbuilt commission, further regulations, appointments, institutional setup, expertise-building, and coming-into-force orders. This is the most Ottawa possible version of urgency.

The combination here is almost impressive. C-36 manages to centralize too much and deliver too little at the same time. Meanwhile the cleaner version is just sitting on the table. Parliament could have modernized the law, strengthened the Privacy Commissioner, provided a transition period, and brought core rights into force on a date certain. That would still have been complicated. Privacy law is always complicated. But the institutional story would have made sense.

Instead, C-36 makes privacy modernization dependent on the birth and maturation of a broader digital regulator that exists only if another controversial bill passes first. That is the machine. C-36 is not C-34. C-36 is not C-22. But it now sits in a landscape shaped by both.

I have already written at length about [Bill C-34](/writing/bill-c-34) and [Bill C-22](/writing/bill-c22-is-a-mess), so I will spare everyone the full tour again. The short version is that C-34 answers a real child-safety problem with, among other things, age-verification and age-estimation infrastructure for ordinary internet access. C-22 answers real investigative problems with a lawful-access framework whose breadth, timing, and trust demands are largely a mess of the government’s own making.

Neither bill begins from an imaginary concern. That is what makes this so frustrating. C-34 is plausible because children really do need protection online. C-22 is plausible because law enforcement really does need lawful ways to investigate serious crime. C-36 is plausible because Canada really does need modern privacy law.

The danger is what happens when all three plausible things are considered together.

C-34 would enact the *Digital Safety Act* and the *Digital Safety Commission of Canada Act*, creating a Digital Safety Commission with powers, duties, and functions under the *Digital Safety Act*. The bill’s summary describes duties around regulated services, digital safety plans, harmful content, children’s safety, complaints, transparency, and other online services that may later be made subject to duties by regulation.

C-22, introduced earlier in this Parliament, is the *Lawful Access Act, 2026*. Its summary says it would amend multiple Acts to modernize the timely gathering and production of data during investigations and would create a framework to ensure that electronic service providers can facilitate authorized access to information under the *Criminal Code* or *CSIS Act*.

Then C-36 arrives as a privacy bill, points at the unbuilt C-34 regulator, and says: that.

I want to be clear: this is not paranoia or conspiracy theory slop. If you are here to argue that the Carney government is secretly creating an authoritarian surveillance state, you can kindly fuck off. This is just reading the table of contents.

C-36 itself also contains consent exceptions. Some of these are ordinary. Some are necessary. Consent is not magic. Everyone who has used the modern internet knows that the current consent model is often a pantomime in which users click through unreadable banners so a company can later say the magic word "consent" in a boardroom. A law that relies entirely on consent will fail because consent has already been operationally exhausted.

So yes, C-36 allows certain activities without knowledge or consent. That is not automatically scandalous. It includes business-activity exceptions, and it also contains a long set of other exceptions for investigations, breaches, legal requirements, government requests, law enforcement, national security, defence, and international affairs.

Some of that is normal. Some of it is necessary. Privacy law cannot be written as though fraud, abuse, subpoenas, investigations, and emergency disclosures do not exist. But this is also where modern privacy law becomes less a wall than a permission architecture.

The question becomes: which actor can collect, use, disclose, retain, review, audit, certify, exempt, or request information under which conditions, with which safeguards, under whose oversight, subject to what internal or external process? That is not automatically bad. It is often better than the old world. But it is still a form of governance where the question becomes less "should this infrastructure exist?" and more "has the infrastructure filed the correct paperwork?"

That is especially dangerous around age assurance. C-34 requires age-verification or age-estimation measures for regulated services that provide access to pornographic content, and it also contemplates age-verification or age-estimation measures for regulated social media services specified by regulation, designed to prevent people under 16 from having an account or otherwise being registered with the service. The bill's table of provisions lays out the minimum-age provisions, the age-measures provisions, and the Commission structure that would sit behind them.

That sounds responsible. It also means that, to protect children from social media, the state is encouraging or requiring infrastructure that can determine whether users are children. Those are not the same thing. You cannot simply say "age verification" and then handwave away the architecture. Age assurance does not occur in a meadow. It requires systems. It requires flows. It requires documents, signals, credentials, estimates, device attestations, facial analysis, third-party providers, parental controls, account-level restrictions, audit trails, or some combination of the above. Even when designed with privacy in mind, it changes the texture of the internet. It makes anonymous or low-friction access less normal. It creates pressure toward identification-like infrastructure.

Then C-36 arrives and says children's data is sensitive, cross-border transfers need privacy impact assessments, automated decisions need explanations, and the same expanded Commission is going to oversee privacy and data protection. This is the contradiction at the heart of the whole thing.

The state wants to protect privacy, but it also wants to regulate the internet through systems that require more information about users, more classification of users, more compliance records, more platform-level determinations, and more regulator-facing documentation. Privacy becomes both the right being protected and the thing being processed in order to prove compliance with the protection regime.

This is how privacy gets laundered. Not destroyed. Laundered. The state does not say, "we are abolishing privacy." The platform does not say, "we would like to identify everyone." The regulator does not say, "please build a surveillance layer." Everyone says the opposite. Everyone says data minimization. Everyone says proportionality. Everyone says retention limits. Everyone says privacy impact assessment. Everyone says destruction after use.

And yet, at the end of the process, there is still more machinery for determining who people are, what category they belong to, whether they are permitted to access a service, what risks they present, what harms they may encounter, and whether the platform’s handling of all that information satisfies the state.

The individual experience of privacy is not just a statutory right. It is the practical ability to move through digital life without constantly being sorted, checked, inferred, scored, verified, warned, blocked, appealed, and explained.

C-36, in isolation, understands part of that. It recognizes inferred and de-identified information as still privacy-relevant. It limits retention. It creates access rights. It gives people some ability to understand automated decisions. It puts safeguards around sensitive information. But the machine around C-36 is very interested in sorting, checking, inferring, verifying, warning, blocking, appealing, and explaining.

That is why institutional design matters. A privacy regulator should have a privacy-first institutional culture. It should wake up in the morning suspicious of unnecessary data collection. It should have an almost annoying reflex toward minimization, access rights, proportionality, deletion, accountability, and individual autonomy. A digital safety regulator has a different centre of gravity. It is concerned with harms, risks, duties, content, platform design, compliance, children, complaints, emergency measures, transparency, and mitigation. Those are real concerns, but they pull in different directions. Safety regulators often want more visibility. Privacy regulators often want less collection. Safety regulators ask whether enough is being done. Privacy regulators ask whether the thing should be done at all.

All of these questions matter. That doesn't mean the same machine should always be asking both. This is the difference between regulatory coordination and convergence. 

Regulatory coordination can be good. The internet does not map neatly onto departmental silos. A privacy complaint can also be a competition issue. A platform-design issue can also be a consumer-protection issue. A safety issue can also be a data-governance issue. Agencies shouldn't have to communicate by throwing PDFs over a wall. But there is a difference between "these agencies should talk to each other" and "privacy should become one division inside a new digital safety and data protection apparatus."

The Privacy Commissioner being outside the digital safety regulator matters because external friction matters. Consultation with an independent privacy watchdog is not the same as internal privacy review. An external office can say no in public. It can disagree institutionally. It can make the conflict visible. It can be annoying from the outside. An internal division can produce a memo. Sometimes that memo will be good. Sometimes internal review will catch problems. Sometimes the combined regulator may genuinely improve coordination.

But presence is not power. A privacy function inside a broader safety regulator may be present at every meeting and still lose every important fight. It may be consulted early and overridden quietly. It may become the part of the institution that helps make intrusive systems compliant rather than the part that asks why those systems exist at all.

There is a very Canadian belief that a problem has been solved once it has been assigned to a commission with a mandate, annual reports, ministerial arrangements, public consultations, and a five-year statutory review. This optimism is not always wrong. Canada is, for better and worse, a country held together by competent administrators, semi-independent agencies, vibes of responsible government, and a national belief that if we put the right people in a room with enough binders, civilization may continue. Sometimes this works. 

But digital infrastructure is not a normal policy domain. Bad digital law doesn't stay politely inside its statutory box. It becomes procurement requirements. It becomes product architecture. It becomes compliance tooling. It becomes terms of service. It becomes internal dashboards. It becomes identity flows. It becomes API fields. It becomes "we need to collect this because Canada." It becomes defaults. And defaults are where rights go to either live or die.

The worst version of C-36 isn't a jackboot kicking down the door of a data centre. The worst version of C-36 is quieter than that. It is a product meeting where someone says, "we should collect this for compliance." It is an internal dashboard that exists because a regulator might someday ask. It is an age-assurance vendor becoming part of ordinary account creation. It is a privacy impact assessment that turns a bad idea into an approved bad idea. It is a Commission guidance document that quietly reshapes the architecture of Canadian internet services. It is an exception that becomes a workflow. It is a safeguard that becomes permission.

That is the thing people tend to miss about digital rights. They are not usually lost in a single dramatic moment. They are lost through reasonable paperwork.

This is why the response to C-36 should not be theatrical panic. No one should be complaining on social media that Confederation has fallen. The bill is not obviously stupid. It contains many things Canada genuinely needs.

But it should also not be passive relief. It is not enough to say, "finally, a privacy bill." It is not enough to see the phrase "fundamental right of privacy" and stop reading. It is not enough to see children's information treated as sensitive and declare victory. It is not enough to see privacy impact assessment requirements and assume the infrastructure underneath them is harmless.

The right response is more annoying than that. Support the privacy modernization. Interrogate the consent exceptions. Narrow the government-disclosure pathways. Demand clearer reporting on state access. 

Do not tie urgent privacy reform to the birth of an untested digital super-regulator. Do not treat C-34's still-contested regulator as reusable machinery before Parliament has even approved it. Keep private-sector privacy enforcement with the existing Privacy Commissioner, or at minimum explain in public why that office is being displaced. Separate, or at least structurally firewall, the privacy function from the digital safety function. Be extremely suspicious of age-assurance infrastructure, even when wrapped in privacy language. Do not let "the Commission may be satisfied" become a substitute for democratic debate. Do not let regulation carry the hard questions Parliament does not want to answer in public. Do not let the existence of safeguards become proof that the underlying system is acceptable.

And above all, stop evaluating each digital bill as if it arrived from space.

C-36 is not just C-36. It is C-36 after C-22. It is C-36 after C-34. It is C-36 in a Parliament increasingly willing to create new digital powers, new digital duties, new digital regulators, new digital categories, and new digital compliance systems faster than the public can understand the combined shape of what is being built.

The privacy bill is fine.

The machine around it is not.
