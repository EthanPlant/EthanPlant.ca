Software should not require permission to run.

That sounds obvious. It used to be obvious. Somewhere along the way we stopped expecting it.

Open a modern app on a plane and watch what happens. Spinner. Login screen. “Reconnecting…” Your computer is fully capable of running the software sitting on its own disk, but the software refuses. It needs approval from somewhere else first.

That’s the defining idea behind modern computing: your machine is not the authority anymore.

It runs the code, but someone else controls it.

You’ve probably felt this already.

Maybe Chrome removed an extension you relied on because Google decided it was no longer acceptable. Maybe a notes app locked you out because your session expired. Maybe a service shut down and gave you thirty days to export years of your life before the servers disappeared.

People treat these like isolated annoyances, and they aren’t.

They are consequences of the same systemic design flaw.

We built a world where software is no longer a tool you own. It is a service you access.

And that distinction matters.

A tool does what you tell it. A service decides what you are allowed to do.

Most modern software is a service, even when it’s installed locally.

Your browser updates itself whether you want it to or not. Your phone removes apps remotely. Your smart devices lose functionality after firmware updates. Features appear and disappear without your consent. Accounts determine whether your own data is accessible.

The interface says “personal computer.” The architecture says dumb terminal.

The free software movement saw part of this coming decades ago. In the mid 1980s the Free Software Foundation argued that users should have the freedom to run, study, modify, and share software. They were right. Arguably more-so today.

But the cloud changed the battlefield.

You can have fully open source software that still leaves users powerless if the real authority lives on someone else’s server. A GPL web app running entirely behind a centralized service still controls the user. The code may be free. The system is not.

The problem now is larger than source code. It is operational control.

We solved this problem once already.

The original personal computer revolution of the 1980s and 90s mattered because it moved authority to the edge. Software ran locally. Your files lived on your machine. Networks extended your computer instead of defining it.

Then the cloud reversed the model.

The pitch was convenience. Synchronization. Simpler deployment. Managed infrastructure. And to be fair, it worked, extremely well. Centralized systems made software easier to build and easier to scale.

But they also concentrated control.

Now nearly everything sits on top of AWS, Google Cloud, or Azure. Identity is tied to accounts. Data is tied to services. Software functionality is tied to policies controlled by companies you do not work for and cannot vote out, and whose interests don’t necessarily align with yours.

We normalized dependence because the UX was smooth enough.

Until it breaks.

And it always breaks eventually.

That’s the moment people realize they don’t actually own the systems they rely on. They have access, but access can be revoked.

Ownership is simpler.

If you own your software:

    it runs without permission

    your data is accessible locally

    it keeps working if the company disappears

Anything less is conditional.

The uncomfortable part is that we’ve accepted this trade almost everywhere. We tolerate software that stops functioning without a network connection. We tolerate products that remove features remotely. We tolerate systems where years of personal data become inaccessible because of a billing issue or policy change.

Imagine accepting this from physical tools.

Imagine buying a drill that refused to work because the manufacturer’s servers were offline.

Imagine a tractor that refused to start because your monthly subscription ended.

People would call it insane.

Software gets a pass because the degradation happened slowly.

Now AI is pushing this even further.

Your workflows, writing, code, and decisions are increasingly mediated through systems you cannot inspect or run yourself. The more useful these systems become, the more dangerous the dependency becomes with them.

The concerning reality is if your thinking depends on infrastructure controlled by someone else, your autonomy depends on it too.

And that is the real issue here. Not convenience. Not architecture diagrams. Authority.

Who decides whether your software runs?
Who decides whether your data is accessible?
Who decides whether a feature exists tomorrow?

Right now, the answer is usually not you.

That needs to change.

We need a different foundation for software. One where the device is the source of truth again. One where applications work offline by default. One where synchronization is optional infrastructure instead of a hard dependency.

That means building systems differently.

Store data locally in open formats users can inspect and verify. Synchronize by exchanging data between devices instead of forcing everything through a central server. Use cryptographic identity instead of accounts tied to platforms. Design for multiple writers and unreliable networks instead of pretending permanent connectivity exists.

The crazy thing is none of this is hypothetical.

Git already proved the model works more than two decades ago. You can commit, branch, inspect history, and merge changes entirely offline. Then synchronize later. The network helps, but it is not required for the system to function.

That design is resilient because it assumes reality instead of fighting it.

Networks fail. Services disappear. Companies change direction. Users deserve systems that survive those conditions.

Call it local-first. Call it sovereign software. The label matters less than the principle.

If software runs on your machine, you should control it.

Not partially. Not until a policy changes. Completely.

The standard should be simple:

If you have your device, you have your data, and the software continues to work.

We need to stop building systems that require permission to run.

And we need to stop accepting them.
