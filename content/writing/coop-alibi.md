A few weeks ago I discovered a project named [Bridger](https://bridger.social/). Bridger is a small social-networking project currently in beta. It presents itself as an alternative to conventional social media: no advertising, no influencers, no algorithmic doomscrolling, and eventually no outside owners extracting value from the people using it.

The proposed product is built around close friends, extensive personal profiles, quizzes, daily prompts, activities, and tools for turning online interaction into real-world plans. The organization behind it describes Bridger as a future member-owned co-operative. Its public pitch is that social technology should serve relationships rather than harvest them.

This would be an interesting project if it worked as advertised. Unfortunately, it does not.

Bridger is not presently a co-op. It may become one. It has produced a [very lovely page explaining how it might become one](https://beta.bridger.social/co-op/). It has phases, voting buttons, a sustainability simulator, and enough soft institutional language to make the average non-profit consultant briefly levitate.

Today however, Bridger is a founder-controlled pre-seed startup. It has a Founder/CEO. It has developers, designers, moderators, and marketers working without pay. It has a waitlist, projected revenue, operating expenses, product releases, and plans to compensate selected workers once sufficient money exists.

What it does not have is present co-operative ownership. Instead, it promises that members may eventually receive meaningful control after volunteers have built the software, users have populated it, paying members have proven the model, and the existing leadership has determined that a collection of extraordinary exit conditions have been satisifed.

## The Discord Argument Wrote the Essay

The most damning criticism of Bridger came from its own team, in real time, while the organization was attempting to explain why one person could delete working software built by unpaid contributors.

One of Bridger's developers publicly quit on June 9. He said he had traded sleep for development time, fixed forty-one recorded bugs, removed two security issues and built several major features without receiving a cent. Those features included events, communities and a private feed.

The feed wasn't TikTok, or some algorithmic engagement engine shoving ragebait into the nervous system until somebody bought protein powder. According to the developers, and from screenshots shared in the Discord, it was a private stream of updates from friends. Something closer to a shared journal than an infinite public scroll.

The events system responded to an actual use case: a coordinator trying to organize groups of between fifteen and fifty people outside Instagram and TikTok. The developer said the direction had been discussed with the community team. It had been brought to beta testers. The testers wanted it. The developers built it and deployed it. Then Brant, Bridger's Founder and "Acting CEO" removed it. Not after a vote, or a design review, or any kind of discussion about whether the feature could be changed to better fit Bridger's anti-addiction goals. He apparently removed it unilaterally.

In the ensuing argument, Bridger's social media manager attempted to defend the arrangement:

> We need someone who is in charge. We can't have a bunch of people doing their own vision because then it likely would not become cohesive in the final project.

Fine. Projects require direction. Somebody must coordinate work, settle disagreements and prevent five developers from independently designing six incompatible applications.

Then, she explained why that authority belongs unquestionably to Brant:

> He came up with the idea so it should be him 100%.

And why everyone else should accept it:

> We have to remember that that is the goal and that we are volunteering our time for the good of that mission.

That message contains the entire organizational model. Brant receives complete authority because he had the idea. Everyone else provides free labour because they believe in the mission. This is described as different from ownership, although every useful property of ownership is present. Brant determines direction. Features should be approved by him. Work that conflicts with his preferences may be removed. Contributors may advise, but he decides.

The distinction between "person who owns the project" and "person who has one hundred percent authority over the project" is doing no practical work. I can only describe it as corporate structure by euphemism. The social media manager also warned that ideas should be approved by the group, "especially Brant", so that work would not be wasted.

The developer's response was immediate:

> Let's stop sugarcoating this with toxic positivity.

The work had been approved, he said. The developers had discussed it. A community representative had discussed it in meetings. Beta testers had requested it. The team had not gone rogue. Brant was the person who bypassed the process. The developers were accused of creating an unauthorized feature that leadership reluctantly had to remove. Their account was that the feature passed through Bridger's actual informal approval process, and the Founder/CEO later deleted it without consulting anyone.

He then stated the contradiction plainly: 

> If one person has 100% unilateral authority to silently delete days of working code without a single conversation, that is not a co-op.

He called the appeal to volunteerism for what it was:

> Calling us "volunteers working for a noble mission" while expecting us to just shut up and accept our hard work being wiped on a whim isn't appreciation. It's a demand for free labor.

Another developer tried to narrow the dispute to poor communication. That was charitable. A communications and marketing member was less so:

> What you just literally said is the equivalent of corporate offices giving a pizza party when they're upset.

The cheerful thank-you message did not alter the power structure. It merely put an orange heart emoji beside it. Bridger's contributors were being told that their work was incredible, their mission was noble, their ideas were valid and their presence was deeply appreciated. They were also being told that Brant remained entirely in charge and that they should have obtained his approval before doing work he had already deleted.

She later continued:

> If this was a co-op, Brant would've dropped the title and full say, and be more inclusive of members opinion, time, and construction of what everyone wants. If a majority say they want something, then it doesn't matter what Brant says because it's a co-op.

That is nowhere near a complete theory of co-operative governance. A majority of Discord users should not necessarily decide every implementation detail. Co-operatives can have executives, delegated authority, product leads and procedures that prevent every disagreement from becoming a plebiscite. But the principle underneath the message is correct. A co-op's leadership is accountable to the institution, the institution is not accountable to the founder.

Bridger had inverted that relationship. The community could vote, suggest and volunteer so long as its decisions remained compatible with Brant's. When they conflicted, the Founder/CEO's authority prevailed. She finished with the obvious conclusion:

> If Brant wants only what he wants in the app, then he should hire and pay people to do that, not take everyone's free labor, tell them it's a co-op where their opinion matters, and proceed to just not do that.

The problem was the bargain. If Bridger was Brant's product, he could define the product and hire people to implement it. If Bridger was a volunteer open-source project, contributors could work under a clear maintainer model and a licence guaranteeing their right to fork. If Bridger was a co-operative, members and workers would possess enforceable rights in the institution. Instead, Bridger selected the most useful feature from each structure. Founder control, volunteer labour, co-operative legitimacy. When challenged, its defenders attempted to reconcile the arrangement through appreciation.

Then the developer wrote the rest of the essay.

## The Developer Had Already Written the Indictment

The developer's longer response was furious. It may be the most scathing indictment of a project I've ever seen an open-source contributor make. 

He rejected the suggestion that the events and feed systems had been built without approval:

> I didn't just sit in a dark room and build this blindly; <REDACTED> and I heavily discussed this direction, and it was mutually agreed upon.

The event feature was based on explicit demand from somebody trying to coordinate groups of fifteen to fifty people. He said he took the concept to beta testers, received enthusiastic support, implemented it and deployed it. Then Brant removed it without prior communication. 

Bridger's later release page would describe group activities and event planning as "not built yet," as though they had never progressed beyond a roadmap bullet. The developer's account says the opposite. The feature existed, the decision to remove it was the part hidden from the eventual release history. 

This leaves Bridger with an uncomfortable choice. Either his account is materially false, in which case the project should correct it with a clear development history, or the Founder/CEO unilaterally removed working software that had passed through Bridger's actual community process.

He then moved from the feature dispute to the organizational structure:

> If one person has unilateral authority to destroy a team's hard work on a whim, we aren't partners in a mission, we are just being treated as an unpaid workforce to build someone else's personal playground.

Bridger's published economics page now makes it difficult to dismiss. The project lists a Founder/CEO, developers, designers, moderators and marketers. Every role is currently unpaid. Volunteer participation explicitly creates no automatic entitlement to employment, compensation, equity or leadership.

He also continued with the product argument. He stated he had shared the project with fifty-three classmates. Only two actually joined. The response, according to him, was immediate:

> If I don't have something to replace my scrolling habit, why should I use this?

The private feed was supposed to provide that replacement. It gave people updates from friends without public follower counts, algorithmic recommendations or the pressure to chase engagement. Remove it, and Bridger becomes what he called, "a glorified quiz platform." Other people inside the Discord independently called it a ghost town, a pile of forms, BuzzFeed, and a data-collection agency. This was not an outside critic imposing a hostile interpretation on an innocent beta. The very people building and using Bridger were already asking why it retained the questionnaires while removing the features that gave anyone a reason to return.

He later criticized the legal and ethical reality of the current state of affairs. His legal conclusion was too categorical. A centralized organization does not automatically transform every unpaid contributor into an employee. Classification depends on the real working relationship, the jurisdiction and the applicable law. The ethical point however survives intact. Bridger cannot use "co-op" to imply shared ownership, "Founder/CEO" to justify unilateral authority and "volunteer" to deny contributors any resulting claim.

Those labels describe three very different things. A volunteer project says people may contribute without expecting ownership. A startup says founders and workers occupy different economic positions. A co-operative says members hold enforceable governance and economic rights. Bridger recruited people with the third bargain, governed them through the second and answered their complaints with the first.

## The Board is Phase 3. The CEO is Phase 0.

Bridger's co-op model lists the roles required to operate the project: Founder/CEO, Developer, Designer, Moderator, Social Media/Marketing. Every role is marked "currently volunteer". Notably, there is no board. There's no elected chair. No treasurer. No member representative. No privacy officer. There is, however, a CEO.

This is not in it of itself a scandal. Co-operatives can employ chief executives. There's nothing suspicious about that. The executive normally reports to a board created under the organization's governing documents and elected according to the rights of its members.

At Bridger, the executive office already exists. Meanwhile the board is left to the distant future. Nothing says the eventual board appoints the CEO. Nothing says it can remove him. Nothing says the title expires when the co-op is established. Nothing says the CEO reports to the membership. The project begins with a standing executive and may eventually install democracy around him.

Its governance roadmap doesn't allow members to elect a board until "Phase 3". That tells you which part of the institution is real. Suppose Bridger reaches Phase 3. Does Brant then report to the board? Can the board dismiss him? Who owns the domain, source code, database, and other assets before then? Who transfers them to the co-op? Under what agreement? What happens if Brant unilaterally decides the conditions for Phase 3 haven't been met, even if they clearly have?

Bridger doesn't even hide the logic. A tooltip explaining why the board is the last step states:

> Electing a board is the highest-stakes governance step. It comes last, once the co-op is financially sustainable or large and members reliably turn out to vote.

That sentence gives away the entire structure. Democratic control is being withheld precisely because the decisions are important. Members are allowed to vote on quiz themes and prompts while the project is fragile. They can only elect the people controlling the money and strategy only after their labour

## Everyone Is A Founder Until Ownership Comes Up

"Phase 0" users are described as the "founding cohort". The site explains they are more than just beta testers because they'll help ratify the charter and pricing:

> Members here are the founding cohort. They get hands-on early access and a real hand in shaping the rules the co-op will launch under.

> What makes them founders, not just testers: they ratify the charter and the pricing.

That is an extraordinary definition of founder. Founders normally create an institution and hold some enforceable interest in it. This is reasonable, founding anything requires extraordinary time and risk, maintaining some lasting interest in it is an entirely reasonable expectation. Bridger's founding cohort receives early access, the ability to suggest ideas, and the opportunity to approve documents produced under a process controlled by the existing leadership.

Meanwhile, participation creates no entitlement to equity, compensation, or leadership. The users are founders, but they receive no ownership. The developers build the product, but they receive no ownership. The moderators accept responsibility for keeping a social network used by teenagers safe, but they receive no employment protections. The marketing team creates the audience, but it receives no claim on the resulting institution. Everyone is a founder when Bridger wants emotional commitment, but only Brant is the Founder when authority is assigned.

The future co-operative performs the motivational role that equity normally performs in an early-stage company. Contributors are encouraged to believe they are building something that will eventually belong to them. Unlike equity, that belief grants no present ownership, no enforceable vote, no claim on future revenue and no protection if leadership changes course.

## Democracy Arrives After the Business Works

The roadmap does not gradually transfer meaningful control from a small team to the community. It postpones all meaningful forms of control until Bridger is already successful. Phase 0 members may suggest features, vote on releases, discuss pricing and ratify a future charter. Phase 1 is called the "Full Co-op." Under the Full Co-Op, members still can't elect the board, amend the charter, or receive any profit sharing. They get to do exactly one thing, vote on, "quiz themes, activities and daily prompts." Bridger has apparently designed a democratic institution in which members gain the right to choose the homework before they gain the right to choose who governs them.

Phase 2 adds "discounts and member perks", with absolutely no elaboration on what that means. 

Only in Phase 3 are members permitted to elect the board and participate in major financial and strategic decisions. 

The sequence is straightforward. Volunteers build the application. Users test it. Members begin paying dues. The membership proves the business model works. The organization becomes financially sustainable. It maintains growth, retention, and participation for at least six months. The members may be allowed to elect a board. The community must remove the commercial and operational risk before receiving control.

In an ordinary startup, the people taking that early risk negotiate equity. At Bridger, they are explicitly told that their contributions create no entitlement to it. This is not democratic leadership unfolding carefully. It is a founder-controlled incubation period followed by a conditional promise to consider democracy at some arbitrary point in the future after everyone has de-risked the enterprise.

## Phase 2 Requires a Small Miracle

The conditions for leaving Phase 2 are so demanding that they may never be meaningfully reached.

There are two paths. The first requires dues to cover operating expenses and at least one full-time team member earning a living wage. This must remain true for six consecutive months, while at least twenty percent of paying members participate whenever something goes to a vote. The second requires paid membership to remain "well past break-even," retain more than seventy per cent of members for six months and maintain the same voting threshold.

Read separately, these conditions sound prudent. Together, they form a constitutional deadlock disguised as a sustainability milestone. Bridger's documentation conveniently leaves out the exact numbers, but gives us enough that I actually did the math.

Bridger's economic simulator starts with 1,072 users on the waitlist, annual dues of thirty dollars, and an assumption that five percent will become paying members. That gives us eighty-five members and $2,553 in annual revenue.

Because Brant has suggested Colorado as the likely home of a future co-operative, a reasonable benchmark is the [MIT Living Wage Calculator's](https://livingwage.mit.edu/states/08/) 2026 statewide estimate for one adult with no children: $26 per hour, or $54,080 for a full-time year. Add Bridger's existing $7,352 in annual expenses and the employer share of Social Security and Medicare—7.65 per cent, and the minimum annual requirement is about $65,569. This still excludes unemployment insurance, workers' compensation, equipment, benefits and every other ordinary cost of employing a person.

At thirty dollars per year, Bridger needs 2,186 paying members. At its own five percent conversion assumption, it needs 43,720 users. Its current waitlist, again, has 1,072. Assuming every user on the waitlist becomes an active member, Bridger must grow its userbase by nearly twenty-five times before one person can be paid a bare living wage under the assumptions Bridger itself chose. Even if every current waitlist signup paid, Bridger would bring in $51,060 annually, more than $14,500 short.

The twenty percent turnout condition then requires at least 438 of those members to vote whenever Bridger puts something before them. Bridger must therefore reach the revenue scale of a real subscription business while retaining the political participation rate of a small activist organization.

And this only pays one person a bare minimum wage. The economics page lists developers, designers, moderators and marketers performing as much as 129 combined hours of work per week. Paying the low end of those published ranges at the same living-wage rate would require roughly $51,000 annually, or about 1,701 paying members. Paying the midpoint would require around $123,000, or about 4,102 paying members. Paying the high end would require approximately $195,000, or about 6,504 paying members. At Bridger's five-percent conversion assumption, the midpoint means a userbase of more than 82,000 people.
 
The alternative path is worse in a different direction. Current non-labour break-even is only about 246 paying members. At five-per-cent conversion, that requires approximately 4,920 active users. But the roadmap does not say "break-even." It says "well past break-even" and leaves that term undefined with no clear messaging on who decides that bar.

Suppose "well past" means double the published non-labour break-even. Bridger would need about 491 paying members, 9,820 users and ninety-nine voters per ballot. That would produce less than $15,000 in annual revenue. Every developer, moderator, designer and marketer could remain unpaid.

Path A therefore requires an extraordinary growth event before one worker can receive a living wage. Path B may allow the organization to cross the threshold while paying nobody at all,provided the Founder/CEO accepts that the undefined phrase "well past" has been satisfied. The same people whose authority is supposedly temporary interpret the conditions required to end it.

## The Board Cannot Actually Come Last

There may also be a much more basic problem with this roadmap, it's probably illegal. I need to make the disclaimer here, I am not a lawyer. I am especially not well-versed enough in Colorado business law to make any concrete claims, take this section with that in mind.

Brant has floated two possible legal structures for this co-op:

> I am leaning towards creating a co-op license in Colorado. They are the most friendly to co-ops. Or we structure an LLC in the style of a co-op.

There is no generic "co-op licence" that activates democracy mode. Colorado offers several formal co-operative structures, including a limited cooperative association created by filing articles of organization with the Secretary of State.

On the obvious limited cooperative association route, the board cannot be a Phase 3 feature. Colorado law requires the association to have a board of at least three directors once it has at least three members, and its affairs must be managed by or under that board's direction. After formation, either the articles name the initial directors or the organizers appoint them. Those directors then hold the organizational meeting and adopt the initial bylaws.

So Bridger's phased model cannot literally mean that the co-op has no board until Phase 3. Under their legal obligations it only means that the members do not elect the board until Phase 3. Someone must control Bridger during the years in which volunteers build the software, users supply their data, members begin paying dues and the project attempts to cross its extraordinary sustainability threshold. Under the Colorado structure, that control would belong to an initial board selected by the organizers.

If Bridger instead forms an LLC "in the style of a co-op," the legal contradiction becomes cleaner. It would be an LLC. Perhaps the operating agreement would promise member votes later. Perhaps Brant could write co-operative principles into it. Perhaps the arrangement would eventually transfer meaningful authority.

Colorado permits the word "cooperative" to be used by an entity genuinely operated on a cooperative basis, even if it is not organized under the co-operative statutes. That doesn't turn every LLC with a future voting roadmap into a co-op. Colorado also gives qualifying entities and members the ability to enforce restrictions against improper use of the term. An LLC controlled by Brant until the product becomes financially successful, after which paying users may receive broader voting rights, would be legally possible with careful drafting.

It would also be exactly what this piece says it is: A founder-controlled company promising conditional democracy later.

## The Adult in the Room Was an Unpaid Developer

The developer who resigned was not just a developer emotionally attached to the features they had built. Across Bridger's discord, he repeatedly appears as the person asking questions a real organization should have assigned to accountable officers.

When the topic of its privacy policy and Terms of Service came up, he said the project needed community input, legal research, accounting and an actual organizational structure before launch.

Brant's answer meanwhile was simply:

> I say we just make it as private and secure as possible.

This is not, how can I say, competent privacy governance. It is what somebody says before handing the problem to the nearest competent volunteer.

When the team discussed infrastructure, he estimated server capacity, uptime, storage and costs.

When hardcoded PostgreSQL credentials were discovered in the application source, he treated the situation as a critical incident, assigning it a CVSS score of 9.1, I ran the calculation myself and agree with this assessment. His report said the credentials potentially allowed full read, write and delete access to the database.

Brant's response when this was disclosed was:

> All the emails and data is encrypted so they can't pull any data, though it did pull all rows with the encryptions.

> Just proves we are all about security being #1!!

The developer had to explain that full database access was not merely a confidentiality problem. An attacker could modify or delete records. They could inject malicious data. They could bypass application-level validation. They could disrupt the service by changing database state. The unpaid developer had to explain to the Acting CEO that information security includes integrity and availability.

[When I raised concerns about the collection of religion, sexuality and other highly sensitive information](/writing/bridger/), he again treated the issue seriously. He said the project needed proper threat modelling, asked the lead developer for clarification on the encryption setup and warned that, as far as he knew, those values were not yet encrypted and were being stored in plaintext. That is an extraordinary contrast with Bridger's public privacy policy, which says staff can only ever see encrypted or hashed versions of user information. The person closest to the implementation was warning that sensitive values might remain plaintext.

He also produced formal documents, including a written incident report for the exposed database credentials. This wasn't an open-source developer writing application code, he was manufacturing the institutional machinery Bridger otherwise did not possess. Security lead, infrastructure planner, incident commander, compliance researcher, policy drafter. All unpaid. The official organizational chart lists none of those offices. The work still existed and needed to be done. It had simply accumulated around the one volunteer behaving as though failure might have consequences.

Then Brant deleted his work and told him that he was free to leave. Bridger lost the person who kept having to explain to the Founder/CEO what operating Bridger actually involved.

## The Privacy Policy Describes an Organization That Does Not Exist

Bridger's privacy policy says it collects names, usernames, email addresses, birthdays, quiz results, session tokens and hashed IP addresses. That inventory appears materially incomplete compared with the onboarding process, which requested substantially more information about users' relationships, education, employment, beliefs and private lives.

The policy says email addresses and birthdays are encrypted and, "never readable by us." That confuses encryption at rest with organizational access control. If the application uses an email address, some process must be able to recover it. The useful questions concern key custody, production privileges, logs, backups and administrative access. Saying "AES-256-GCM" does not answer any of them.

The policy says Bridger staff can see only encrypted or hashed data. Who are Bridger staff? Which legal entity employs them? Who owns the hosting account? Who controls the keys? Who may access production? Who handles a deletion request? The policy provides only an email address.It describes the practices of a coherent institution while the people building Bridger are publicly arguing about whether it belongs to Brant or to the community.

The Terms of Service have the same defect. Users supposedly enter an agreement with "Bridger," but Bridger is never legally defined, and to date, no legal entity actually exists under that name. The Terms say users grant Bridger a licence to display their content. Which person or entity receives that licence? They say "we" may suspend accounts. Who is "we"? They promise that data is removed when an account is deleted. Which institution is responsible for fulfilling that promise? The effective date will be added before launch, despite the document acknowledging that real people are already using the beta. Bridger has contractual promises before it has clearly identified the party making them.

## The License Is Based On Vibes

Bridger says its software is open source, with its homepage even linking to its Codeberg. 

Internally, the team is still discussing what that actually means. One developer suggested choosing an existing open-source license. Brant has publicly stated he leans towards AGPL. One user meanwhile suggested:

> we can just make our own terms
> the license isnt some boilerplate
> we can modify agpl

Another contributer proposed calling the result, "Bridger Modified AGPL". This is the software-licensing equivalent of discovering municipal law and deciding to write a slightly modified building code for your shed. A licence is in fact not a decorative `LICENSE` file added when the README looks lonely. It determines who may use the software, who may modify it, whether contributors may fork it, what obligations apply to hosted versions and whether the project can later be transferred into another organization.

Modifying the AGPL would create a new bespoke licence, not a personalized edition of the AGPL. It would need to be drafted carefully, named differently and evaluated for compatibility and actual open-source compliance. Bridger was discussing this casually while already accepting code from multiple unpaid contributors, with every current legal question being answered on vibes and one developer's assumption of what the law says.

There's actually an extremely serious intellectual property issue here. Bridger accepts code from unpaid contributors. There is currently no licence, no copyright assignment, and no contributor agreements. Its own economics page explicitly warns contributors that volunteering does not make them employees. Under the ordinary rule, the person who writes copyrightable code owns it. Sending that code to a repository does not silently transfer the copyright to the person calling himself Founder/CEO. A transfer generally needs to be written down and signed.

Bridger may have permission to use the contributions. A developer who submits a patch, watches it merge and continues working on the project has probably not preserved the right to suddenly claim that Bridger was never allowed to run the code at all. Copyright law can recognize an implied non-exclusive licence based on what the parties actually did. That is not the same as ownership.

Bridger cannot safely take code owned by several volunteers and announce that the entire repository is now licensed under the AGPL, "Bridger Modified AGPL," a proprietary commercial licence or whatever legal instrument the Discord invents next. It can licence what it owns. It can pass along rights that contributors have actually granted. It cannot manufacture rights it never received.

This creates an extraordinary trap. The project tells unhappy developers that they are free to leave and build something else. Without a licence, those developers may not be permitted to redistribute the combined repository they helped create. At the same time, Bridger may not possess the rights needed to redistribute that same repository under a new licence either. The contributors may be unable to fork the project. The project may be unable to cleanly relicense the project. Everyone has spent months writing code into a legal cul-de-sac.

The problem becomes worse when Bridger incorporates. A future Colorado co-operative or LLC does not automatically inherit copyrights held personally by Brant and every other contributor. Those rights need to be assigned or properly licensed to the new entity. That means identifying every copyright holder. Finding every contributor. Determining which code each person wrote. Obtaining agreement on a licence or assignment. Potentially negotiating with people who left after accusing the Founder/CEO of exploiting their labour. And rewriting anything whose author refuses.

This is the sort of issue that sits unnoticed during a friendly beta and then detonates during investment, incorporation, acquisition, commercialization or a serious governance dispute. Any competent due-diligence process will ask whether the company or co-operative owns the software it claims as its principal asset.

Bridger's answer appears to be maybe. The volunteers seemed fine with it at the time. One of those volunteers built major systems, wrote the incident reports, challenged the Founder/CEO publicly and quit in anger. He may still own the copyright in every original line he contributed.

## We Might Pay Some of You Later

During the developer revolt, Bridger's social media manager said:

> I think the plan was that once we start making money people working on it would be paid.

That is not how permanent volunteer service for a community institution is normally described. That is exactly how an unfunded startup describes deferred payroll. Bridger expects the unpaid team to help create a revenue-producing product. If it succeeds, some contributors may later be selected for compensation.

But selected by whom? There's no elected board. There's no compensation committee. There's no treasurer handling the money. There's no published hiring process. The economics page lists factors compensation "should" consider: organizational need, available resources, responsibility, expertise and time commitment. Those answer's all sound sensible while answering nothing. Who determines organizational need? Who decides which role is essential? Who gets paid first? Does the Founder/CEO receive the first full-time living wage required to exit Phase 2? Does previous unpaid work count for anything? Can the developers who built the product simply be replaced once money arrives? Nothing guarantees that the people who absorb the early risk receive the later benefit.

## The Apology Changed Nothing

When the argument escalated, Brant initially became defensive. He emphasized his all-nighters. He posted the contribution graph showing him as the top contributor to the repo. This is at some level understandable, it can be extremely difficult when people start accusing you of mismanaging your own project.  Other community members had to tell him directly that this was not how to respond. 

He eventually produced a longer apology document. The underlying structure did not change. He was sorry the conflict had become painful. He cared about the contributors. He appreciated their work. He wanted to discuss how to proceed. He did not concede that developers possessed meaningful decision-making rights. He did not establish a binding governance process. He did not recognize their labour through ownership. He ultimately returned to the position that contributors who wanted a different direction were free to build something else. The apology addressed the emotional consequences of unilateral control without relinquishing unilateral control.

## One Step Short of Calling It a Scam

I still cannot establish that Bridger was deliberately designed as a deception. It may be the product of sincere people who like the language of community ownership but have no meaningful understanding of ownership, governance, labour, privacy, licensing or institutional accountability. That possibility is not flattering. At a certain point, incompetence becomes structurally indistinguishable from exploitation. The developers perform unpaid work under the belief that they are building a community project. Brant exercises the authority of a startup founder. The public is told that member control will arrive after conditions that may never be satisfied. The organization plans to collect dues. The people building it are explicitly warned that their work grants no compensation, equity or leadership claim.

Features built by those volunteers are deleted and later described as "not built yet." Members can veto a release but do not control which disputed decisions reach the ballot. Its public privacy claims conflict with the internal understanding of the people closest to the implementation. Its most competent technical volunteer leaves after being treated as disposable. Days later, the project faces another security scare. Before any outside article appears, Bridger's own members are already calling it a data-collection agency and asking whether they work for the platform or for Brant. The only permanent governance position already visible is Founder/CEO.

Call it a future co-op if you like.

Today, Bridger is a founder-controlled startup financed with volunteer labour and wrapped in synthetic institutional copy about the democracy it may someday permit.
